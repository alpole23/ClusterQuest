Phosphonate Cluster Detection
Preprint · methods

Two blind spots in automated biosynthetic gene cluster detection, and a pipeline that closes them

A. Polidore, M. H. Medema, W. W. Metcalf

ClusterQuest — github.com/alpole23/ClusterQuest

19 September 2026 · revised 1 November 2026 antiSMASH 8.0.4 · BiG-SCAPE 2.0.1 · GTDB r226 · Pfam 38.2
Abstract

Automated biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) detection is usually treated as a solved preprocessing step. Using phosphonate BGCs as a test case, we show that two independent failures of the standard antiSMASH configuration remove a large fraction of cluster gene content before any downstream analysis begins, and that they are not the same failure. First, the phosphonate detection rule ships a 5 kb flank that is too narrow for this chemistry: 4.2 kb of the characterised HiVir cluster of Pantoea ananatis LMG 5342 lies outside it. Second, antiSMASH performs gene finding only on records with zero CDS features, so a partially annotated deposit is trusted for all of its genes; on the same genome this left 14 of 28 genes of a confirmed phosphonolipid cluster invisible, including both CDP-alcohol phosphatidyltransferases. Correcting both raises the two clusters from 10 and 14 genes to 25 and 37.

We further show the corrections are not independent — recovered genes can match the detection rule, extending the cluster core and shifting the flank by 1.6 kb — and that the two clusters in this single genome require opposite fixes. We describe a pepM pre-screen that cuts antiSMASH compute 5.1× on a dilute taxon without losing a BGC, validate it on a clade contributing none of its references, and report a sensitivity failure that only that held-out clade exposed. All figures regenerate from committed data.

1Introduction

Phosphonates are a structurally diverse class of natural products unified by a direct carbon–phosphorus bond, installed by phosphoenolpyruvate mutase (pepM). Because pepM is obligate, phosphonate biosynthesis is one of the few natural-product classes with a single reliable genomic marker, which makes it an unusually clean system for asking how well automated BGC detection actually performs.

The conventional pipeline — antiSMASH for detection, BiG-SCAPE for gene cluster family (GCF) clustering — is typically run at defaults and its output taken as the substrate for everything downstream: gene-content metrics, family assignment, novelty ranking, target selection. We report that at defaults this substrate is systematically incomplete, in two ways that compound, and that the incompleteness is invisible: a truncated or under-annotated cluster produces no warning and scores as a minimal cluster rather than an unreadable one.

This matters beyond bookkeeping. An earlier version of this work attempted to predict phosphonolipid versus small-molecule product from cluster gene content and obtained an inverted answer on the two clusters whose chemistry is known from laboratory work. The cause was not the metric: all four signals tested had been measured on a cluster the pipeline could not see.

2Results

2.1Two blind spots, measured on one genome

P. ananatis LMG 5342 carries two phosphonate clusters — the pantaphos/HiVir cluster and a confirmed phosphonolipid cluster — in a 2012 GenBank deposit that annotates a minority of their genes. Running all four combinations of detection neighbourhood (5 or 10 kb) and ORF recovery (off or on) isolates each effect and their interaction (Figure 1).

The two clusters require opposite fixes. Pantaphos gains more from the wider window (+9) than from recovery (+4); the phosphonolipid gains more from recovery (+14) than from the window (+8). Neither correction alone is sufficient for both, and they sit in the same genome — so this is not a property of assembly quality or submitter, but of which genes the annotation pipeline happened to call and where the rule core happened to fall.

Why a 2 × 2 and not a sequence

The two corrections interact. Recovered genes can themselves match the detection rule, which extends the rule core, which moves the flank with it: region 2's 3′ boundary shifts 1,566 bp from recovery alone, at both neighbourhood settings. Presenting one correction as applied "before" the other would assert an independence the data does not support.

The recovered genes are not filler. In the phosphonolipid cluster they include the 2-aminoethylphosphonate transaminase, both CDP-alcohol phosphatidyltransferases, a phosphocholine cytidylyltransferase and an MFS transporter — that is, the genes that determine what the cluster makes. In the pantaphos cluster, recovery restores the 175 aa small subunit (LeuD) of a 3-isopropylmalate dehydratase whose 412 aa large subunit was annotated, completing a two-subunit enzyme, plus a GNAT-family N-acetyltransferase.

2.2ORF recovery is targeted, not indiscriminate

A correction that adds genes everywhere would be suspect. Applied across four clades (Figure 2, Table 2), recovery instead fires in proportion to how incomplete the deposit is — and what it recovers tracks that too.

Across the Erwiniaceae set, recovery raises the fraction of cluster CDS carrying an informative product from 38.0% to 78.8% when combined with within-family annotation transfer, over 8,400 CDS in 22 families. On modern complete B. fragilis assemblies it is nearly silent: 82 of 99 recovered genes are annotated "hypothetical" and only three receive any antiSMASH functional call, because the core cluster was already fully annotated in the deposit.

Breadth and value come apart at the top of that table. Enterobacterales is the broadest case measured — 1,303 regions, 56% gaining a gene — and also the least informative: all 1,126 recovered genes are unclassified by antiSMASH and 968 of them are annotated "hypothetical". These are RefSeq assemblies, annotated by PGAP, so what recovery finds is not the missing biosynthetic core of a 2012 deposit but the short and dubious open reading frames a modern pipeline declines to call. A high recovery rate is therefore not by itself evidence that anything important was missing; the P. ananatis and Winslowiella panels, where recovery restores an AEP transaminase and both CDP-alcohol phosphatidyltransferases, are the cases that matter, and at 29% of regions gaining a gene they are the minority of their own clade rather than the typical case.

2.3A pepM pre-screen removes most of the compute at no cost in BGCs

Because every phosphonate BGC carries a pepM, a genome without one cannot contain the target. Establishing this costs ~1.4 CPU-s against antiSMASH's ~42 CPU-s on the same genomes, which is what makes order-scale analysis tractable. The screen is deliberately detection-neutral by design — it cannot find more BGCs, only fewer — so the evidence that matters is that the BGC count is unchanged (Figure 3).

On Erwiniaceae — 2,863 genomes, 11% carrying a pepM — antiSMASH falls from 1,984 to 386 CPU-minutes, 5.1×, for a screen costing 69 CPU-minutes: a return of 23 to 1. Both arms find the same 335 regions in the same 22 families. On P. ananatis — 344 genomes, 56% pepM-positive — the same comparison gives 1.7× (529 to 311 CPU-minutes, on realtime × cpus) and the same 226 regions either way, all 226 identical base for base. That ratio is valid within its own pair, but its basis differs from every other CPU figure in this paper and its traces no longer exist, so it is reported here rather than plotted beside them.

The screen therefore pays in proportion to how dilute the taxon is, which is what its mechanism predicts: it removes genomes, so it can only save what those genomes would have cost. We decline to put a trend line through two points. An earlier version of this section reported a monotonic relationship across four datasets, but two of the four were curated probes assembled to test sensitivity rather than taxa sampled for cost, and they supplied both endpoints of the trend; the relationship they described was an artefact of that selection. Only full taxa, each run both ways, are reported here.

Why this figure reports antiSMASH CPU, not total CPU

The screen decides which genomes antiSMASH sees and nothing else, so antiSMASH CPU is the quantity it acts on. Whole-pipeline totals for the Erwiniaceae pair are 2,287 and 498 CPU-minutes, a larger-looking 4.6×, but that figure also folds in ORF recovery (257 → 14 CPU-minutes), which shrinks only because it too runs on the screened subset.

CPU-minutes for Erwiniaceae, and every projected figure in section 2.6, are realtime × %cpu, the CPU actually consumed. The P. ananatis pair above is the one exception in this paper and is labelled where it appears; an earlier version of this note claimed a single basis throughout, which was wrong. A previous version of this work reported 6.5× for Erwiniaceae from a pair whose arms differed in antiSMASH batching, in whether ORF recovery existed at all, and in GTDB-Tk, and which was aggregated as realtime × cpus, the cores reserved rather than used. Re-run as a controlled pair the figure is 5.49× on that same basis and 5.1× on this one. A CPU ratio is not interpretable until its basis is named, and two ratios are comparable only on the same basis and the same code.

2.4A held-out clade exposed a sensitivity failure

Every prior validation of the screen was run on a clade the reference set draws from. Erwiniaceae supplies one of the seven pepM references; the actinomycete set supplies the other six and contains the source strain of one, which self-matches at bitscore 828. Neither can answer whether the screen generalises.

Bacteroides fragilis (phylum Bacteroidota) contributes none of the references, and is held out in BGC space as well: all five characterised reference clusters sit 0.82–0.93 from their nearest B. fragilis BGC, with none inside the 0.30 GCF cutoff. Ground truth from an unscreened arm gave 98 BGC-positive genomes of 136 (143 regions, 19 GCFs) — the largest held-out positive set measured, with no self-match.

The failure

The screen missed 2 of 98. Both scored exactly 0.0 — not a weak hit, no hit at all — while antiSMASH called a phosphonate region in each. Both loci are annotated phosphoenolpyruvate mutase and flagged /pseudo, and NCBI withholds /translation from a pseudogene-flagged CDS. The pepM protein never reached the aligner; the screen was not failing to recognise a pepM, it was never shown one.

The existing partial-annotation guard could not catch this: both genomes are densely annotated overall (763 and 726 CDS/Mb against a 500 CDS/Mb threshold), because density is a whole-genome proxy for what is a single-gene problem.

Translating such a CDS from its own coordinates — keeping internal stops rather than truncating, since a pseudogene spreads its signal across the frameshift — recovers both at bitscore 388 and 310 against a cutoff of 100. In region terms the screened arm found 141 of the unscreened arm's 143 regions before the fix and 143 of 143 after it. These counts are reported here rather than drawn in Figure 3: B. fragilis is a curated held-out probe, not a taxon, and a bar beside a taxon measurement cannot carry the caveat that makes it meaningful.

2.5Clustering at scale trades runtime for memory

BiG-SCAPE's all-pairs comparison is quadratic in both time and memory. Measured to 10,000 BGCs, peak memory fits GB = 1.80 + 2.04 × 10⁻⁷ n², projecting ~2.8 TB at the ~117,000 BGCs a million genomes would yield — a machine most groups do not have. The coefficient is the benchmark's ru_maxrss fit scaled by 1.58: BiG-SCAPE runs eleven processes and ru_maxrss reports only the largest, so summing resident set size across the process tree — counting each shared page once, by its proportional share — gives 1.58× the recorded figure, stably across five sizes. Partitioning on pepM identity bounds this, at a cost (Figure 4).

Known defect

The partition cap quoted in Table 4 — about 16,000 BGCs in a 64 GB allocation — inverts the process-tree fit above, as it should. The shipped partitioner does not: it inverts the unscaled ru_maxrss fit, and so admits about 20,300 BGCs into the same allocation, roughly 1.27× what it holds. Partitioning is off by default, so no result reported here is affected and nothing needs re-running — but the cap is the only thing standing between a large run and an OOM kill, so the figure a reader should plan against is 16,000, not what the code currently derives.

We report this as a trade rather than an improvement, because at every scale we could measure it is a runtime loss. Its value is that it converts an impossible job into a possible one without changing a cluster assignment beyond BiG-SCAPE's own run-to-run noise: identical at 185 and 518 BGCs, and at 1,302 BGCs three of 1,302 members move — the same three that move between two unpartitioned runs of the same input, so the disagreement is the tool's, not partitioning's. The runtime crossover is projected near 10,000–12,000 BGCs, above anything we measured directly.

Reproducibility

BiG-SCAPE 2.0.1 returns different distances for identical input. Its input deduplication is keyed on a Python set of hash strings, so load order follows the per-process hash salt; that order sets record identifiers, which set pair orientation, and pair extension is asymmetric. Between repeat runs 8–14% of pairs differed, by up to 0.18. Pinning PYTHONHASHSEED=0 made repeat runs identical. GCF membership at the 0.30 cutoff was never affected — no distance below 0.5 ever moved — but family centres did move, so representative selection was unstable before this.

CPU-hours throughout this section are realtime × %cpu — the CPU actually consumed, not the cores reserved. The two differ by about a factor of two on this pipeline, and mixing them is how an earlier version of this section came to overstate the screen's value.

Elapsed time used to be a scheduling problem and is now a bandwidth one. At one antiSMASH job per genome and a 20 jobs/min submission limit, submission alone took 833 h against about 50 h of compute. Batching removed that: at 50 genomes per job, and with only the ~108,000 screened genomes reaching antiSMASH, submission is about two hours and compute about nine. What is left is the fetch, which no amount of batching or concurrency helps, because the limit is the link rather than the scheduler: across 2 to 12 concurrent fetches bandwidth moves only 22.4 to 24.7 MB/s, a 10% gain for six times the concurrency, and batches begin failing at eight. The reason not to raise the setting is those failures, not a decline in throughput — an earlier version of this paragraph reported a peak at two concurrent fetches, which was the shape seen by the same superseded WiFi probe discussed below. The only lever left is egress.

The screen is worth 3.1× on the whole projection, because antiSMASH is 75% of it. Two rows move. antiSMASH moves because the screen decides which genomes it sees, which is the point. ORF recovery moves with it — 1,498 to 81 CPU-hours — because it too runs only on genomes that passed, and that is a consequence of screening rather than a second saving to claim credit for. Fetch is unchanged, since a genome must be fetched to be screened. BiG-SCAPE is unchanged, since the screen is detection-neutral and the same BGCs reach it. GTDB-Tk is unchanged because it already ran on BGC-positive genomes only, and those are a subset of what the screen keeps — 300 of the 308 on the controlled pair — so the two filters do not compound.

The screen's own 400 CPU-hours are the largest single cost it adds and are paid on every genome, including the 89% it discards. It still returns 23 CPU-hours of antiSMASH saved per CPU-hour spent, or 27 against every stage it touches.

The GTDB-Tk rows are an upper bound

Both GTDB-Tk figures — 1,108 CPU-hours here, 34 hours per shard in Table 4 — come from a scaling benchmark run with --skip_ani_screen, which the phylogeny module hardcoded at the time. It is now a parameter and the pipeline defaults it to off, meaning the ANI screen runs. That screen classifies most genomes by average nucleotide identity without ever reaching pplacer, and it was measured 3.2× faster on 450 GenBank genomes and about 10× faster on 2,530 RefSeq ones, for taxonomy identical at every rank. So the real figures are smaller by some factor between one and ten, and nothing here measures which: the screen's own cost is dominated by sketching GTDB's reference set, a fixed cost per shard that 21 shards would pay 21 times. The unscreened figure is reported because it is the one that was measured. It is the largest row the projection cannot yet pin down, and it sits on the compute critical path.

Correction

Three earlier versions of this table were wrong, in two distinct ways, and the second way is the one worth learning from. Two of them mixed CPU accounting bases. One gave ~24,100 CPU-hours unscreened, assembled from measurements taken at different times; a second gave ~25,001 with a 4.6× saving. Both drew their antiSMASH rate from a run aggregated as realtime × cpus — cores reserved — while other rows were actual CPU, so the largest row was inflated against the rest by roughly the ratio of allocated to used cores.

The third was rebuilt correctly on one basis, and then priced two stages against the wrong population. It charged GTDB-Tk 1,271 CPU-hours at “~121,000 BGC-positive genomes” — but 121,000 is not a count of genomes. It is the count of BGCs: 333 regions over 2,758 genomes, scaled to a million. The same run carried 108,049 BGC-positive genomes, so a count of clusters had been substituted for a count of genomes, overstating that row by about 15%. It travelled undetected because 121,000 is the right population for BiG-SCAPE, which does consume BGCs. Repriced at the controlled pair's measured rates — 10.5% of genomes BGC-positive, 11.7 BGCs per 100 genomes — GTDB-Tk is 1,108 CPU-hours and BiG-SCAPE 1,020, giving an unscreened total of 15,330 and a saving of 3.1×.

Both errors are the same shape: a quantity used where a different quantity belonged, in a number that carried a rate but not what the rate was multiplied by. Every row in Table 5 now names its population, and every CPU figure in this section names its accounting basis, so that the next such substitution is visible in the table rather than only in the arithmetic.

Storage is set by what is never written, not by what is deleted. Three changes take the projection from 49.6 TB to ~2 TB. Publishing by hard link rather than copy, and dropping the redundant publish of each raw download, removed three of the four copies every genome used to occupy: ~52 to ~16 MB per genome. The remaining factor is the pre-screen. It decides which genomes antiSMASH sees, so at Erwiniaceae's 11% retention the other 89% need never be written at all — the screen runs inside the task that fetches and renames a batch, and a rejected genome is deleted before its output is declared. That leaves ~0.96 TB of retained GenBanks and ~0.97 TB of antiSMASH output, with the per-genome HTML viewer off saving a further 0.13 TB.

Deleting them afterwards does not work, which is why the ordering matters: Nextflow re-publishes from its work directory on resume, silently undoing any prune, and pruning published output races with tasks still writing it. For the same reason the download itself is batched — resolving the taxon to an accession list first and fetching in small groups — so that peak disk is set by the batch size rather than by the size of the taxon. Downloading a million genomes into one directory before the first screen can run would need 8.7 TB whatever is deleted later.

3Limitations

  • The held-out clade is one species. Its 98 true positives cluster tightly around a modal bitscore of 514, so this is closer to one well-replicated test than to 98 independent ones. The pseudogene finding is robust to this; a claim that the screen generalises across divergent pepMs would need a genus- or phylum-scale set.
  • The 10 kb neighbourhood is tuned to one cluster, and 10 kb is a floor rather than a round number. It was chosen because it captures HiVir completely, which is now measured: across the 169 complete members of the pantaphos family the cassette reaches 9,189–9,792 bp past the rule core, so 8 kb and 9 kb both clip its terminal ATP-grasp. The flank is symmetric, so the window also admits ~10 kb of upstream context the cluster does not contain — and that context dominates the comparison: a median of 4 Pfam domains lie inside the rule core against 19 across the whole region, so 79% of what BiG-SCAPE compares is flanking sequence. The value is a floor for this chemistry, not a general claim.
  • Family counts are an upper bound on distinct chemistries. Because the comparison is dominated by flank, and flank is conserved within a host species but divergent between them, BiG-SCAPE separates clusters that share an identical rule core. Three of the four pantaphos families are Pantoea — the fourth is Photorhabdus/Xenorhabdus — and those three carry the same core domain content and differ only in chromosomal context; the two largest sit 0.680 apart over the whole region and 0.237 apart over the biosynthetic cassette alone. This is not fixed by the cutoff — the largest family is 186 members at 0.20, 0.30 and 0.40 alike, because BiG-SCAPE 2 assigns families by affinity propagation within a connected component rather than by thresholding, and propagation splits whatever the threshold admits. Reported family counts should therefore be read as an upper bound, and the per-family distance matrix in the pipeline report as the means of checking any particular pair.
  • Partitioning is validated at 185–518 BGCs, not at the scale that requires it, and has not been run on a cluster scheduler.
  • Gene categories are antiSMASH's own. Region GenBank files carry no Pfam accessions, so 15 of 28 genes in the illustrated cluster are unclassified — including the CDP-alcohol phosphatidyltransferases that determine the product. Product-keyword classification was tried and rejected: it inverted on the two laboratory-confirmed clusters, because serine hydroxymethyltransferase matches methyltransferase.
  • Predicting phosphonolipid versus small molecule remains unsolved. With the genes restored, the confirmed lipid and confirmed non-lipid are nearly identical in domain content, differing only in CDP-alcohol phosphatidyltransferase copy number — one example per class, which is not signal.

4Methods

Pipeline. Nextflow DSL2. Genomes are retrieved from NCBI, screened for pepM, annotated with antiSMASH 8.0.4 restricted to the phosphonate rule, clustered with BiG-SCAPE 2.0.1, and placed with GTDB-Tk 2.6.1 against GTDB r226. Databases are version-pinned and the pins are written into every run's output; an unpinned URL resolves once and is never re-checked, which had previously left this pipeline classifying against a seven-month-old taxonomy with no record of it.

Detection neighbourhood. antiSMASH exposes no option for a bacterial neighbourhood, so an idempotent, atomic patch is applied to the installed rule file before each invocation. The value enters the antiSMASH parameter hash only when set, so a run at the tool's own value reproduces results produced under it.

ORF recovery. A protein pool is built from the analysed genomes; genes absent from the deposit are recovered by homology and supplied to antiSMASH as GFF3. This is necessary because antiSMASH runs gene finding only on records with zero CDS features.

pepM pre-screen. DIAMOND against seven curated pepM references at bitscore 100. Annotated genomes take a blastp path over their proteins; unannotated ones take blastx over contigs. The two paths were verified to make identical calls on all 341 genomes where both could run. A CDS lacking /translation is translated from its own coordinates, with internal stops retained as X.

Comparisons. BGCs are matched between runs on genome plus region filename, never on file hash: antiSMASH stamps a run date into every region GenBank, so no two runs agree byte for byte. Clustering comparisons use co-membership and the adjusted Rand index rather than family identifiers, which are autoincrement values recording write order and do not name the same family across databases.

Data and code availability

Pipeline, figure-generating scripts and the extracted comparison data are at github.com/alpole23/ClusterQuest (MIT).
